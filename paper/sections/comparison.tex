\section{Comparing the Estimators}
\label{sec:comparison}
Table~\ref{tab:estimators} compares the four estimators in
Eqs.~\eqref{eq:score}--\eqref{eq:poststratified} at the level of a single
update. The score-function estimator
uses the objective value at one mixture draw $Z$. The implicit and
post-stratified estimators also need the gradient with respect to that point.
Stratification evaluates both at a separate draw $Z_k$ from each component
and weights the resulting values by the mixture probabilities.
The single-draw and stratified objective estimates are
\begin{equation*}
 f = f_\theta(Z)
 \qquad\text{and}\qquad
 \bar f = \sum_{k=1}^K \pi_k f_\theta(Z_k).
\end{equation*}
We write $h$ for the input gradient at $Z$. The subscript in $f_k$ and $h_k$
places the value and gradient at the draw from component $k$.

To read a gradient column, choose the parameter $\lambda$ to update.
A logit $\alpha_j$ controls how often component $j$ is selected. A coordinate
$\eta$ of $\phi_k$, such as a Gaussian mean, controls where component $k$
places its mass. Add the \emph{direct term} to the \emph{sampling term} for
that parameter. The direct term differentiates the objective while holding
the sampled points fixed. The sampling term accounts for the change in
their distribution.
For the quadratic objective in the Gaussian mean row, the mean enters only
through sampling, so that row gives the entire update.

\paragraph*{Matched sampling budgets.}
With $K$ objective evaluations, stratification samples every component once.
With the same budget of independent mixture draws, the chance of selecting
component $k$ at least once is $1-(1-\pi_k)^K$. All $K$ draws can miss a
component with a small weight.

\paragraph*{Differentiating the component samplers.}
Stratification differentiates each component sampler with the noise that
generated its draw. Post-stratification starts from one observed point and
asks how each component could have produced it. Write $v_k$ for the
component's sample velocity at fixed noise and $e_k$ for the noise that
would reproduce $Z$.
\begin{equation*}
 v_k(e) = \partial_\lambda T_k(e;\phi_k),
 \qquad
 e_k = T_k^{-1}(Z;\phi_k).
\end{equation*}
Stratification uses $v_k$ at the original noise $\varepsilon_k$.
Post-stratification uses it at $e_k$ and weights the contribution by $r_k$,
the probability that component $k$ generated the observed point.

\paragraph*{Holding percentiles fixed.}
The implicit estimator follows the point whose percentile stays fixed as
the mixture changes. For a scalar draw, this percentile is the mixture CDF
$F$ at $Z$. The scalar entries also use the component CDF $F_k$ and the
mixture density $q$ at the same point. For a vector draw, $S=S_\theta(Z)$
collects the percentiles of successive coordinates, each conditional on the
preceding ones. A single triangular solve gives the vector $u$ used in every
parameter's sampling term.
\begin{equation}
 (D_z S_\theta(Z))^\top u = h,
 \qquad
 \text{sampling term for }\lambda = -u^\top\partial_\lambda S_\theta(Z).
 \label{eq:implicit-adjoint}
\end{equation}
The partial derivative on the right holds $Z$ fixed. The extra work in the
implicit column comes from computing the rank derivatives. For diagonal
Gaussians, evaluating either the mixture density or the rank map costs
$O(Kd)$. Forming the dense Jacobian takes $d$ reverse passes through that
map, followed by an $O(d^2)$ solve~\citep{burroni2026mixture}.

\paragraph*{Which updates coincide?}
In the two-Gaussian example, shifting a component mean shifts each of its
draws by the same amount. At a fixed point, the derivative of that
component's CDF with respect to the mean is the negative of its density.
Substitution into the scalar implicit formula gives the mixture draw a
velocity of $r_k(Z)$. Post-stratification gives the same velocity by
weighting the component's unit shift by its responsibility. Multiplying
this shared velocity by the slope $-Z$ of the quadratic objective gives
\begin{equation*}
 \widehat g_{\mu_k} = -r_k(Z)\,Z,
 \qquad
 \mathbb E[\widehat g_{\mu_k}] = -\pi_k\mu_k .
\end{equation*}
For a logit, post-stratification and the zero-baseline score-function
estimator agree on the same draw for any objective.

\paragraph*{Variance.}
The variance comparison starts with a gradient estimate that keeps the
sampled label $C$ and the noise $\varepsilon_C$ that generated its point.
With that information the estimate is
\begin{equation}
 G = \partial_\lambda f + h^\top v_C(\varepsilon_C)
     + f\,\partial_\lambda\log\pi_C .
 \label{eq:label-gradient}
\end{equation}
The three terms account for the direct derivative, the motion of the draw
within its component, and the change in its label probability.
Stratification fixes the component counts, leaving only variation among
draws within each component. Post-stratification averages $G$ over the
possible labels of the observed point. With finite second moments, the
variance of that conditional average is at most the variance of $G$
\citep{burroni2026mixture}.

\paragraph*{At the exact posterior.}
For observed data $x$ and a fixed model $p$, the evidence lower bound
(ELBO) averages a log density ratio over mixture draws. Holding the sampled
point fixed gives the negative of the mixture score as its direct derivative.
\begin{align*}
 f_\theta(z) &= \log p(x,z)-\log q_\theta(z),\\
 \partial_\lambda f_\theta(Z) &= -\partial_\lambda\log q_\theta(Z) = -s_\lambda .
\end{align*}
This direct derivative has mean zero. Sticking the Landing (STL) omits it
and keeps the derivatives due to sampling~\citep{roeder2017stl}. When the
mixture equals the posterior, the density ratio equals the evidence $p(x)$
at every point. The objective is then the constant $L=\log p(x)$ and its
sample slope is zero. The stratified and implicit estimates vanish on every
draw. The score-function estimate and the post-stratified logit estimate can
still vary with the draw.
