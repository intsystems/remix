\section{Problem Setup}
Let $\theta\in\mathbb R^m$ contain the parameters of a mixture density on
$\mathbb R^d$. Our objective and mixture are
\begin{equation}
 \mathcal J(\theta)=\mathbb E_{q_\theta}[f_\theta(Z)],
 \qquad q_\theta(z)=\sum_{k=1}^K\pi_k(\theta)q_{k,\theta}(z),
 \qquad \pi_k>0,\quad \sum_k\pi_k=1.
 \label{eq:objective}
\end{equation}
We assume differentiable densities that are positive on the interior of a common,
parameter-independent support, with integrable derivatives allowing
differentiation under the integral. Pathwise identities additionally require
vanishing boundary flux; variance comparisons require finite second moments.

The responsibility $r_k(z)=\pi_kq_{k,\theta}(z)/q_\theta(z)$ is the conditional
probability that component $k$ generated $z$. We write
$a_k=\nabla_\theta\log\pi_k$ for the weight score and
$s_\theta(z)=\partial_\theta\log q_\theta(z)$ for the mixture score.
For the objective, $h_\theta(z)=\nabla_z f_\theta(z)$ describes its sensitivity
to the sample, while $d_\theta(z)=\partial_\theta f_\theta(z)$ captures direct
parameter dependence at fixed $z$.

Component samples take the form $Z_k=T_{k,\theta}(\varepsilon_k)$, where
$\varepsilon_k\sim\rho$ has a parameter-independent distribution. Stratification
uses derivatives of these maps. Post-stratification also uses their inverses;
for this method, we assume differentiable component bijections onto the common
support and write $V_k(z)$ for $D_\theta T_{k,\theta}$ evaluated at
$\varepsilon_k=T_{k,\theta}^{-1}(z)$.

For a fixed model $p(x,z)$, variational inference maximizes the evidence lower
bound (ELBO), with integrand
\begin{equation}
 f_\theta(z)=\log p(x,z)-\log q_\theta(z),
 \qquad d_\theta(z)=-s_\theta(z).
 \label{eq:elbo}
\end{equation}
The score has zero expectation, so omitting $d_\theta$ preserves the expected
ELBO gradient. This is the STL modification \citep{roeder2017stl}.
In automatic differentiation, it freezes the parameters of $q$ inside the
log-density term while preserving its derivative through $z$. We denote an
operation that preserves a value and stops its derivative by
$\operatorname{sg}$. For general objectives, the estimators retain $d_\theta$.

As a running example, consider a mixture of two univariate Gaussians,
$q_\theta(z)=\pi\mathcal N(z;\mu_1,\sigma_1^2)
+(1-\pi)\mathcal N(z;\mu_2,\sigma_2^2)$, where
$\pi=\operatorname{sigmoid}(\alpha)$ and $\sigma_k>0$.
We use the objective $f(z)=-z^2/2$, which favors samples close to zero.
Writing $m_k=-(\mu_k^2+\sigma_k^2)/2$ for its expectation in component $k$,
we obtain
\begin{equation}
 \mathcal J=\pi m_1+(1-\pi)m_2,\qquad
 \partial_\alpha\mathcal J=\pi(1-\pi)(m_1-m_2),\qquad
 \partial_{\mu_k}\mathcal J=-\pi_k\mu_k,
 \label{eq:running-example}
\end{equation}
where $\pi_1=\pi$ and $\pi_2=1-\pi$. Thus, ascent moves each mean toward zero
and shifts weight toward the component with the larger expected objective.
The known gradient lets us follow how each estimator recovers the same
quantity from different random samples. Here $h(z)=-z$ and $d(z)=0$;
the ELBO additionally has the direct density derivative described above.
